What Does Wealth Data Aggregation Cost an Advisory Firm?

PRICING · 7 min read

Ask three advisory firms what they pay for data aggregation and you will get three different answers — and none of them will match the invoice. The largest aggregators in the business do not publish prices. Plaid lists three plans, Pay as You Go, Growth, and Custom, and describes the features of each in careful detail. It lists no dollar figure for any of them. The first 200 API calls are free. Everything after that is a negotiation, and the negotiation starts with the vendor knowing the market and the buyer not knowing it.

That opacity is the first cost of wealth data aggregation, and it is the one firms notice last. A firm shopping for a data pipe cannot price-shop it the way it price-shops a custodian or a CRM. There is no rate card to compare, no public schedule to anchor on. What exists instead is a set of pricing shapes — per connected account, per household, per API call, flat monthly platform fee — and a set of industry-reported ranges that vendors will neither confirm nor deny. The bill a firm eventually pays is the product of which shape it chose, how many accounts it connects, and how hard it negotiated. The bill it should have budgeted for is larger, because the invoice is only the middle of the cost.

Three ways to charge for the same pipe

The per-account model is the one most firms meet first. Plaid bills by product, and each product has its own unit: a one-time fee per successful link, a monthly subscription per connected account, or a flat fee per API call. Industry research compiled in 2026 puts basic Transactions access at roughly $0.20 to $0.50 per connected account per month on a startup plan, falling to $0.05 to $0.15 at volumes above 100,000 connected accounts. Auth verification runs $0.30 to $1.50 per check. A per-link fee of $0.50 to $2.00 applies each time a client connects an account — and 15 to 25 percent of links require reconnection every year, which means the firm pays the link fee again for accounts it already had.

The per-household model looks simpler and usually is not. Enterprise aggregators built for wealth management — Envestnet's Yodlee above all — sell the way enterprise software has always sold: a platform fee plus a per-user charge. Reported ranges put the platform fee at $5,000 to $15,000 a month, with per-user fees of $0.10 to $0.50 per active user a month. The attraction is predictability: a firm with 800 households knows its monthly number. The trap is the definition of an active user and the minimums buried in the contract. A household with six accounts at four institutions costs the aggregator six connections and costs the firm whatever the contract counts — and the two numbers rarely match.

The flat-fee model is the newest arrival and the most honest about what it is. Teller, the API-only challenger, publishes its price: free for the first 1,000 enrollments, then $0.05 per active connection per month. No negotiation, no sales call. At 10,000 connected accounts that is $500 a month against $2,000 to $5,000 for the equivalent Plaid footprint at reported startup rates. The catch is coverage. Teller reaches a fraction of the institutions Plaid does — Plaid connects to more than 12,000 — and a wealth firm whose clients hold accounts at small banks, credit unions, and 401(k) recordkeepers will find the cheap pipe stops short of the accounts that matter.

The line items that never appear on the invoice

Aggregation pricing is only the first of four costs. The second is reconnection churn. Bank logins expire, passwords change, multi-factor prompts interrupt the feed, and every broken link is a client who has to re-authenticate or an account that goes dark. At 15 to 25 percent annual reconnection, a firm with 5,000 linked accounts re-pays link fees on 750 to 1,250 of them every year — and absorbs the staff time spent chasing clients to reconnect.

The third cost is reconciliation labor, and it dwarfs the subscription. A 2024 survey by Aite-Novarica found that wealth management firms spend an estimated 15 to 20 percent of their operations budgets on data reconciliation — humans checking whether the numbers match and fixing them when they do not. For a $5 billion RIA, that is potentially $2 million to $3 million a year spent on work that should not need doing. The aggregator's fee buys the data's arrival. It does not buy the data's correctness. Cost basis arrives in different conventions from different custodians. Corporate actions are reported differently by Schwab and Fidelity. Alternative investment values arrive quarterly, restated after the fact. Somebody on staff reconciles all of it, and that somebody's salary never appears in the aggregation line item.

The fourth cost arrived in 2025, and it is still being negotiated. JPMorgan Chase began charging aggregators for access to customer data — a mix of fixed and per-request fees — and by late 2025 had signed updated contracts with the intermediaries behind more than 95 percent of the data pulls on its systems, including Plaid, Yodlee, Morningstar, and Akoya. People familiar with the matter told American Banker the fees run to fractions of a cent per data pull. That sounds trivial until it is multiplied: JPMorgan recorded 1.89 billion API requests from aggregators in June 2025 alone, and reporting at the time put the annual exposure for the largest users at as much as $300 million. Plaid told American Banker it would not pass the new fees on to its 7,000 fintech and bank clients, and its renewed JPMorgan agreement left customer pricing unchanged. A Stripe executive called the proposed fees "extortionate" in a letter to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. The question for advisory firms is not whether the fees exist — they do — but where they surface next: in a platform price increase, a feature that quietly disappears, or a per-connection surcharge that arrives with the next renewal.

The counterargument: the cheap pipe is the expensive one

There is a case for paying more, and it starts with what breaks. Plaid repaired 6,121 broken bank integrations in 2025 — a measure of how fragile the plumbing is even at the largest scale, and of how much maintenance a subscription actually buys. A feed that breaks on a Friday and is not fixed until Tuesday costs a firm more in staff hours, client calls, and delayed reporting than a month of aggregation fees. The aggregator that charges more and fixes breaks faster can be the cheaper choice on a true-cost basis, and the firm that chose the lowest bidder learns this the week its largest client's held-away accounts go dark before a quarterly review.

The same logic applies to the data-quality work the invoice never mentions. The old 1-10-100 rule of data management — it costs $1 to prevent an error, $10 to correct it after entry, and $100 to act on it without catching it — is the real pricing schedule for wealth data. A firm that buys aggregation and skips validation is buying the $100 version of every mistake. The subscription is the smallest number in the true cost of the data layer. The reconciliation payroll is the largest. The price of a bad number reaching a client is the one that cannot be budgeted at all.

What this means for wealth firms

Price aggregation per household, not per account, and include the reconciliation payroll in the comparison. A vendor that looks expensive at $0.50 per account and delivers clean, validated data can cost less than a vendor at $0.10 that delivers raw feeds your staff spends hours fixing. Ask every vendor three questions before signing: what share of your connections are API-based rather than scraped, how do you charge for reconnections, and what happens to our price when a bank starts charging you. The answers will tell you whether you are buying a pipe or renting a problem. And budget for the invoice to grow — the era when the pipes were free ended in July 2025, and the firms that planned for metered data will be the ones that are not surprised when the meter starts running.
