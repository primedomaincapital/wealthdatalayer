Open Banking vs. Screen Scraping: What the Data-Pipe Reversal Means for Advisors

OPEN BANKING · 8 min read

For a decade the deal was simple, and nobody read the fine print because there was no fine print. An aggregator logged in to a client's bank account as if it were the client — username, password, and all — copied the balances and transactions off the screen, and handed them to an app. It worked on almost any account, it cost the bank nothing, and the client never saw it happen. The industry called it screen scraping, and it built the entire fintech economy on top of it. Then the banks started charging for the door, the regulators started rewriting the rules, and the question advisors are asking now is not whether scraping dies. It is what replaces it, what it costs, and which accounts it still cannot reach.

The replacement is open banking: standardized APIs, tokenized access, no passwords stored anywhere. The Financial Data Exchange — the industry body writing the standard — reported about 114 million customer connections running through FDX-aligned APIs in April 2025, up from 76 million a year earlier and 16 million in 2021. Early 2026 figures put the number above 130 million. APIs now carry roughly two-thirds of permissioned data traffic in the United States. The direction is settled. The details — coverage, reliability, cost — are where advisors will feel the difference.

Two ways into an account

Screen scraping works by impersonation. The aggregator stores a client's bank credentials, logs in as the client, and reads the web page the way a human would. Its strength is reach: if an account has a website, it can usually be scraped, including the long tail of small banks, credit unions, and recordkeepers that will never build an API. Its weakness is everything else. A bank that redesigns its login page breaks every scraper overnight. A multi-factor prompt stops the feed until a human intervenes. And the credentials sit in a third party's vault — the security exposure that made the whole method indefensible once a better one existed.

An API connection works by permission. The client authorizes the bank to share specific data with a specific recipient, the bank issues a token, and the token — not a password — moves the data. Nothing is stored that can log in as the client. The feed is structured, documented, and versioned, which means it breaks less often and fails more gracefully when it does. The cost is coverage: an API exists only where a bank built one, and the banks that built them first were the largest ones. Akoya, the aggregator created and owned by major banks and brokerages, is API-only by design — no credential storage at all — and it is the clearest statement of where the bank-owned side of the industry wants this to end.

Coverage: who each method reaches

On coverage the scoreboard is split by account type, and the split favors different winners. APIs win the mainstream: the largest banks and brokerages built FDX-aligned connections first, and the Financial Data Exchange's membership now exceeds 200 organizations. Plaid's network reaches more than 12,000 institutions, and most of its data now arrives through bank APIs that Plaid helped banks build. Yodlee, the enterprise incumbent, lists more than 19,000 data sources.

Scraping still wins the edges, and the edges are where wealth management lives. 401(k) recordkeepers, small trust companies, alternative investment administrators, and the long tail of community banks and credit unions are the accounts least likely to have an API and most likely to matter to a high-net-worth household. The average affluent household holds accounts at four to six institutions, and the fifth and sixth are usually the ones no API reaches. A firm that moves entirely to API connections gains reliability on its largest accounts and loses visibility into the accounts its clients actually ask about. The honest coverage answer in 2026 is a hybrid: APIs where they exist, scraping where they do not, and a vendor honest enough to say which of a firm's accounts fall in each bucket.

Reliability: what breaks, and how often

On reliability the API wins, and the evidence is the repair bill. Plaid repaired 6,121 broken bank integrations in 2025, with 52 percent fixed automatically by another app in the network. That number is mostly a scraping number: credential-based connections break when banks change their sites, and banks change their sites constantly. API connections break too, but they break on a schedule — a versioned change, a documented deprecation — rather than on a Tuesday morning when a login page moves.

The counterweight is that API failures, when they happen, are harder to work around. A scraper can be patched in hours by reading a changed page. An API that a bank has throttled, repriced, or shut off cannot be patched at all; it has to be renegotiated. JPMorgan's fee schedule is the sharpest version of this: the largest bank in the country decided its data is a product, signed updated contracts with the intermediaries behind more than 95 percent of the data pulls on its systems, and left the aggregators negotiating as buyers. The pipe got more reliable and less free in the same year.

Cost: the reversal

Cost is where the reversal bites. Scraping was free to the bank and expensive to the aggregator, which paid for the maintenance. APIs are expensive to the bank — the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's own sample of large providers reported annual developer-interface costs of $2 million to $47 million, with a $21 million median — and the banks have decided to recover it. JPMorgan recorded 1.89 billion API requests from aggregators in June 2025 alone, with only 13 percent linked to direct customer activity; the bank attributed $50 million in fraud losses to the traffic. Its proposed fees ran from 5 cents for a balance check to $1.25 for a new account link, and reporting at the time put the annual exposure for the largest users at as much as $300 million.

So far the cost has stopped at the aggregator. Plaid told American Banker it would not pass the JPMorgan fees on to its 7,000 fintech and bank clients, and its renewed agreement left customer pricing unchanged. A Stripe executive called the proposed fees "extortionate" in a letter to the CFPB. But the regulatory floor under free access is gone: the CFPB finalized its Section 1033 open-banking rule in October 2024 with a ban on fees, a federal court blocked it, and the bureau's rewrite — sent to the White House budget office in August 2026 — reopens whether banks may charge at all. If the rewrite permits fees permanently, the data layer becomes a metered utility, and the meter runs on every connection an advisory firm holds.

The counterargument: scraping earned its reputation

None of this is a defense of scraping. The method's security record is poor — Plaid paid $58 million in 2021 to settle a class-action lawsuit over collecting excessive data and misrepresenting how it was used — and the shift to tokenized access is a genuine improvement whatever it costs. The advisors who complain loudest about data fees are often the ones whose clients' logins were sitting in a third party's vault. Akoya's model shows that bank-owned, API-only, tokenized access can work without credential storage at all, and the CFPB recognized the Financial Data Exchange as the first standard-setting body under the 1033 rule in January 2025, which means the market is converging on the FDX specification regardless of what happens to the rule itself.

Still, the transition has a cost that its champions understate. Every account that moves from scraping to API is an account that has to be re-permissioned by the client, and every re-permissioning is a moment a client can say no — or simply never get around to it. Coverage figures count connections, not clients who completed the consent flow. The firms that will feel the transition first are the ones with the oldest, least digital client bases, which is to say the firms with the most assets.

What this means for wealth firms

Inventory your connections by method, not by vendor, and ask your platform for the API share of each account type — the number will be high for checking and brokerage accounts and low for 401(k)s and alternatives, and the gap is the work ahead. Expect held-away alts and retirement accounts to stay on scraped connections longest, and budget staff time for the re-permissioning wave as banks retire credential-based access. Watch the CFPB's 1033 rewrite the way you would watch a custodian fee schedule, because that is what it is becoming. And build direct custodian feeds where you can: the firms that win the next decade of the data layer will be the ones that own their connections instead of renting them from an aggregator that rents them from a bank. The pipes were never free. The bill was just deferred — and the meter is now running on the method that replaced the free one.
