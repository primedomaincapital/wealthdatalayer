The $4 Trillion Reconciliation Problem: Why Wealth Management Runs on Broken Data

WEALTHTECH DATA QUALITY  ·  5 min read

The email arrived at 6:47 on a Tuesday morning. A family office CIO in Greenwich opened it to find that his firm’s consolidated performance report — the one his principals reviewed over breakfast — showed a $14 million position in a private credit fund that didn’t exist. Not a rounding error. A phantom position, conjured by a data feed that had mapped a capital call notice to the wrong account. It took three people, four hours, and one very uncomfortable phone call to unwind.

Nobody lost money. But the CIO lost something harder to replace: confidence that the numbers on the screen matched reality.

This is the open secret of wealth management in 2026. An industry overseeing more than $4 trillion in U.S. registered investment adviser assets alone — the SEC’s latest Form ADV data puts the figure at roughly $4.7 trillion across 15,000+ firms — runs its daily operations on data infrastructure that would embarrass a mid-sized e-commerce company. Portfolio accounting systems built in the 1990s. Custodial feeds that arrive in seventeen different formats. Alternative investment data that still moves by PDF and email.

The aggregation era solved the wrong problem

The first wave of wealthtech innovation — Plaid, Yodlee, MX, and their successors — solved data access. A firm could pull a client’s held-away accounts into a dashboard. That was genuinely useful. Plaid alone now connects to more than 12,000 financial institutions.

But access isn’t infrastructure. Knowing a number exists somewhere is different from knowing it’s right, knowing what it means, and knowing it will be right again tomorrow. The aggregation APIs return raw positions. They don’t reconcile. They don’t normalize cost basis methodologies across custodians. They don’t tell you that Schwab reports a corporate action differently than Fidelity, or that the alternative investment administrator restated last quarter’s NAV after you already ran performance.

BlackRock understood this two decades ago. Aladdin, its risk and portfolio management platform, now oversees more than $21 trillion in assets — not because BlackRock is the biggest asset manager, but because it built the data layer everyone else rents. Aladdin doesn’t just show positions. It normalizes, validates, and reconciles them against a single security master. That boring, invisible work is worth more than any dashboard.

The wealth management industry never built its Aladdin. Instead, it accumulated point solutions: Advent for accounting, Black Diamond for reporting, Orion for billing, eMoney for planning, Salesforce for CRM. Each excellent at its job. None of them agreeing on what the client’s net worth actually is.

The cost of disagreement

A 2024 survey by Aite-Novarica found that wealth management firms spend an estimated 15 to 20 percent of their operations budgets on data reconciliation — humans checking whether the numbers match, and fixing them when they don’t. For a $5 billion RIA, that’s potentially $2 to $3 million a year spent on work that shouldn’t need doing.

The pain concentrates in three places. First, alternatives. Private equity, private credit, hedge funds, and real estate now represent 15 to 25 percent of high-net-worth portfolios, up from single digits a decade ago. Yet alternative investment data arrives quarterly, on different schedules, in non-standard formats, often requiring manual entry. A firm with 200 clients in 40 different private funds is running 8,000 manual data processes per quarter.

Second, held-away assets. The average high-net-worth household maintains accounts at four to six institutions. Each has its own feed quirks, its own corporate action conventions, its own definition of “settled.” Reconciliation across custodians is where phantom positions are born.

Third, planning integration. Financial plans built in eMoney or MoneyGuidePro pull from portfolio systems that may be days or weeks stale. A plan that says a client can retire at 62, built on data that’s wrong by 8 percent, isn’t a plan. It’s fiction with nice charts.

Who’s building the layer

The opportunity has attracted three categories of builders. The incumbents — Advent (now part of SS&C), Black Diamond (Fiserv), Orion — are modernizing their data models and opening APIs, trying to become platforms rather than applications. SS&C’s acquisition spree has given it an enormous installed base to convert.

The infrastructure startups — firms like Snowflake’s financial services practice, Databricks, and a crop of wealthtech-specific data platforms — are selling the picks and shovels: cloud data warehouses purpose-built for the quirks of financial data, with reconciliation engines and security masters as managed services.

And the aggregators are moving upstack. Plaid’s acquisitions and product expansions suggest it understands that raw connectivity is commoditizing. The value is in what happens after the data arrives.

The counterargument deserves airtime: maybe the problem isn’t infrastructure but incentives. Custodians have little reason to standardize feeds — proprietary formats create switching costs. Fund administrators charge for data services that would be free if reporting were standardized. The industry’s data mess persists partly because several large players profit from it. Technology alone doesn’t fix misaligned incentives.

That said, the direction is clear. The firms winning high-net-worth mandates in 2026 aren’t winning on investment performance — that’s increasingly commoditized. They’re winning on client experience, and client experience is downstream of data quality. A firm that can show a client their complete financial picture, updated daily, reconciled to the penny, has an advantage no stock-picking record can match.

The $14 million phantom position got fixed by lunch. But the CIO in Greenwich now asks a question in every vendor meeting that he didn’t ask before: “Show me your reconciliation engine.” The industry’s next decade belongs to whoever can answer convincingly.
