How Long Does It Take to Fix Broken Wealth Data?

DATA QUALITY · 7 min read

Every advisory firm that has tried to fix its data asks the same question in the first meeting: how long will this take? The honest answer is that it depends on which problem is actually being fixed. Cleaning a list of duplicate client records is a project with an end date. Fixing the reason duplicates keep appearing is a change in how the firm works, and it has no end date at all. Firms that confuse the two get a clean database in March and a dirty one by Christmas — and then they pay for the same project twice.

The range of honest answers runs from four weeks to twelve months, and the difference is scope, not effort. A structured cleanup of a single dataset — 10,000 records or more, with defined rules and a business team that reviews quickly — typically takes 4 to 10 weeks. A firm-wide program that covers every dataset, names an owner for each one, and puts checks at the point of entry runs about twelve months for a mid-sized company, with the first visible result inside three months. Both numbers are real. They describe different jobs, and the firms that get burned are the ones that budget for the first and discover they needed the second.

The ninety-day version

The fastest credible fix is a quarter-long sprint on one dataset — the messiest one, the one that undermines the planning cycle or the quarterly report. The sequence is disciplined and public enough to copy. Weeks one and two: scope the dataset and measure a baseline, because the baseline is how the project's return gets proven later. Weeks three through five: audit and profile the data, finding where the errors cluster. Weeks six through nine: deduplicate and standardize — merge the duplicate records, rewrite the descriptions, retire the codes nobody uses. Weeks ten and eleven: validate with the people who actually use the data, because the most common failure of a cleanup sprint is zeal — deleting records that look incomplete but carry a decade of history. Week twelve: lock in prevention, with mandatory fields at entry, a duplicate check at record creation, a named steward for exceptions, and a monthly quality scorecard so decay is visible before it becomes a crisis.

Ninety days is enough for one dataset. It is not enough for a firm. The sprint fixes the records that exist on the day the extract is taken. It does not change what happens the day after, when a new account is opened with a free-text field and a misspelled name. That is why the sprint's final week matters more than the first eleven: without the entry checks and the scorecard, the cleanup is a lawn mowed once.

The twelve-month version

A full program runs in five phases over about a year. Phase one, weeks one to four: kickoff and baseline — a decisions list, a data inventory, and the numbers the firm's leaders will quote later. Phase two, weeks five to ten: owners and rules — a named owner for each dataset, agreed definitions, quality thresholds. Phase three, weeks eleven to twenty-two: a pilot fix on one dataset, cleaned end to end, with checks live at entry and the error rate published weekly. Phase four, months six to nine: extend to three to five more datasets and name the system of record. Phase five, months ten to twelve: embed the routines, train the staff, and decide on tooling so the program runs without outside help.

Two phases slip most often, and neither slips because of technology. Phase two slips because nobody wants to own a dataset. Phase four slips because departments disagree about which system is the record of truth. Practitioners report both running two to four weeks late about half the time, mostly over scheduling and disagreements. A firm planning a cleanup should budget the extra month where the org chart is the obstacle, not where the software is.

The accounts that resist every fix

Some data resists cleanup no matter how long the project runs, because the problem is not in the firm's systems at all. Alternative investments are the worst of it. Private equity, private credit, hedge funds, and real estate now represent 15 to 25 percent of high-net-worth portfolios, up from single digits a decade ago — yet the data arrives quarterly, on different schedules, in non-standard formats, often by PDF and email, and fund administrators restate net asset values after the firm has already run performance. No amount of internal scrubbing fixes a number that arrives late and changes after delivery.

Held-away accounts are the second holdout. The average high-net-worth household maintains accounts at four to six institutions, each with its own feed quirks, its own corporate action conventions, its own definition of settled. Cost basis is the quiet killer here: Schwab reports a corporate action differently than Fidelity, and a firm consolidating both has to choose a convention and defend it. Retirement accounts are the third. 401(k) recordkeepers were built for participants, not for aggregation, and their feeds are among the thinnest and least standardized in the industry. A cleanup project can fix the firm's own records in ninety days. It cannot fix a recordkeeper that has no incentive to standardize.

The scale of the underlying maintenance explains why the work never ends. Plaid repaired 6,121 broken bank integrations in 2025 — connections that had worked and then stopped, because a bank changed a login page or a feed format. If the largest aggregator in the country spends its year repairing pipes, an advisory firm should expect its own data to need the same kind of permanent attention.

The counterargument: maybe do not fix it at all

There is a case for leaving some data alone, and it is stronger than vendors admit. The old 1-10-100 rule — $1 to prevent an error, $10 to correct it after entry, $100 to act on it without catching it — cuts both ways: it is also an argument for preventing errors at the point of entry rather than funding an annual cleanup of errors the firm keeps creating. And a cleanup without governance has a documented shelf life. Without daily controls, master data quality can degrade by 50 to 80 percent within 12 to 24 months after a cleanse, as free-text entries and duplicates come back through routine transactions. Twelve to twenty-four months is one or two budget cycles. A cleanse completed this year can be largely undone before the next roadmap review.

The uncomfortable implication is that a firm that cleans its data every two years is not running a data program. It is running a subscription to the same project, and paying full price each time. The firms that escape the cycle are the ones that spend the cleanup budget on entry controls instead — mandatory fields, duplicate checks at creation, a named steward — and treat the cleanup itself as the last resort, not the plan.

What this means for wealth firms

Budget 60 to 90 days for audit and remediation before any analytical work begins, and do not build dashboards on data that has not been through it — a dashboard built on uncleaned data amplifies the problem rather than surfacing it. Pick one dataset, fix it in a quarter, and use the result to win the argument for the twelve-month program. Name an owner for every dataset before the project starts, because the owner is the deliverable that determines whether the fix survives. And measure quality monthly, in public, with a scorecard the firm's leadership actually reads. A cleanup takes ninety days. Keeping data clean takes a steward, a scorecard, and the willingness to stop bad records at the door. The firms that learn that distinction pay for the project once. The rest pay for it forever.
