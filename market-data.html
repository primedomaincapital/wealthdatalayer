Wealth Data Platform Pricing and Market Data Feed Costs

A wealth platform's data bill has three layers — the terminal, the exchange feed, and the aggregation platform — and each one prices by a different logic.

The terminal: the anchor price of the whole stack

Every wealth-data budget is negotiated in the shadow of one number. A single Bloomberg Terminal seat runs $31,980 a year — $2,665 a month — with multi-seat sites at $28,320 per seat per year ($2,360 a month), according to 2026 pricing coverage, and Bloomberg sells it on a two-year contract rather than month to month. The terminal bundles real-time data, news, analytics, and the messaging network that connects more than 350,000 decision-makers; leaving it costs a desk its counterparties, not just a feed, which is why the price has moved in only one direction.

The exchange layer: priced by the schedule, not the salesman

Below the terminal sits the exchange layer, where prices are public because they have to be: fee schedules filed with regulators. Nasdaq's Depth-of-Book schedule sets TotalView display pricing for professional subscribers at $84.00 per month operative January 1, 2026, up from $80.50 operative January 1, 2025, with non-professional subscribers at $15. For non-display use via direct access, the first 1–39 subscribers run $412 per subscriber per month operative January 1, 2026. The schedule also shows the scale cliff: a firm with 250 or more direct-access subscribers pays $75,000 per firm per month. Broker-published market-data pricing fills in the rest of the shelf: NYSE Network A professional at $45 per month, Nasdaq Network C professional at $25, NYSE ArcaBook professional at $65, and Nasdaq TotalView-OpenView professional at $90, all via Interactive Brokers' published pricing page.

The structure matters more than any single line. A wealth platform pays the terminal price for humans, the exchange schedule for feeds, and a platform fee for aggregation — three different pricing logics stacked into one bill. Firms that audit only the top line miss the middle layer, where subscriber counts and display-versus-non-display classifications quietly decide whether the invoice is thousands or tens of thousands a month.
